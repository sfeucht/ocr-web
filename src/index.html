Mapping from Pixels to Words

In this work, we identify attention heads in vision-language models (VLMs) that are responsible for OCR, which is the task of "reading" text in images. But these heads turn out to be useful for visualizing semantics of image tokens in general!

Specifically, we find a set of attention heads in Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct that, e.g., attend to the word "bike" when prompted to transcribe a word. If we redirect the attention patterns of these heads to different parts of the image, the model will actually verbalize more general information, like "feathers." We use this surprising finding to create a new method that helps us visualize how a VLM might understand the semantics of a particular image, called verbalization lens.

Finding Verbalization Heads

How do we actually find these attention heads? We generate a dataset of images with random words in them, and then prompt a VLM to "Transcribe the word in this image." This is a simple optical character recognition (OCR) task. Then, we look for attention heads at the last token position that are promoting the given word. Specifically, we take the output of each attention head and use logit lens to obtain the probability of the desired word in that attention head's output. Averaging these probabilities across many images, we can obtain an "OCR score" for each attention head. If we then progressively knock out the top percentage of heads based on these scores, we find that they seem to comprise about 10% of attention heads across the four models we study.

Creating our Lens

Our Figure 1 shows what happens when we literally change the attention maps of all verbalization heads to be concentrated on a single token, and then run a full forward pass for Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct. However, in practice, we actually do something more efficient. Repurposing a method from our previous work on language-only models, we collapse the weights of all of these attention heads into a single matrix \(\mathbf{L} \in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}}\), which looks like: \[ \mathbf{L} = \sum_{(l,h)\in\mathcal{H}} \mathbf{W}_O^{(l,h)}\mathbf{W}_V^{(l,h)} \] where \(\mathbf{W}_O\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{head}}},\mathbf{W}_V\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{head}}\times d_{\text{model}}}\) are the output and value weight matrices for a particular attention head \(h\) at layer \(l\), and \(\mathcal{H}\) is the set of verbalization heads we study in this work. But why are we multiplying these weights together? Elhage et al. (2021) refer to \(\mathbf{W}_O^{(l,h)}\mathbf{W}_V^{(l,h)}\) as the "OV matrix" for a given attention head. The intuition for one of these matrices is that it determines exactly what head \((l,h)\) will write when attending to a particular hidden state. It lets us ignore the attention pattern of the head and focus on what that head actually reads and writes.

So, what does it mean to sum together these OV matrices? Let's say that we have a particular residual stream state \(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{model}} \) at layer \(l-1\), and we decide to make all of the \(n\) attention heads at layer \(l\) fully attend to only this hidden state. Then, the contribution of all of these attention heads will look something like this (dropping norms): \[ \mathbf{W}_O^{(l,0)}\mathbf{W}_V^{(l,0)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{W}_O^{(l,1)}\mathbf{W}_V^{(l,1)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} + ... + \mathbf{W}_O^{(l,n)}\mathbf{W}_V^{(l,n)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} \] But then, we can see that this is equivalent to just summing together all the OV matrices first, and multiplying by \(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}\) only once. \[ (\mathbf{W}_O^{(l,0)}\mathbf{W}_V^{(l,0)} + \mathbf{W}_O^{(l,1)}\mathbf{W}_V^{(l,1)} + ... + \mathbf{W}_O^{(l,n)}\mathbf{W}_V^{(l,n)})\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} \] Thus, adding together all of these matrices is actually very natural, at least when heads are in the same layer. The one wrinkle is that we actually make the choice to sum together OV matrices for heads across different layers. This means that, in effect, we are allowing heads at e.g., layer 24 attend to hidden states from layer 0. While this may seem odd, it seems to work well in practice, which corroborates evidence from prior work showing that layers are somewhat interchangeable in LLMs (Todd et al., 2024, Lad et al., 2025). Once we have this matrix \(\mathbf{L}\), we can apply it to any hidden state \(\mathbf{h}\) to reveal what all the attention heads in \(\mathcal{H}\) would output if they were to fully attend to that state. We then simply apply logit lens to \(\mathbf{Lh}\) instead of "raw" logit lens on \(\mathbf{h}\) to obtain the results shown in this work.

While this is conceptually similar to redirecting attention heads in a real forward pass, it is much more computationally feasible, as we only need to cache activations from one forward pass, rather than running a separate intervened pass for every image token. The other advantage is that we can observe how information evolves across layers, showing us that information is often already present in layer 0.

Editing Image Perception

To show that our lens \(\mathbf{L}\) is reading from features that causally effect the model's outputs, we edit within the subspace read by these verbalization heads, and find that these edits affect model descriptions of images. For example, we can "edit" an image representation to cause Qwen to describe a photo of an ant as an iPod. On a high level, our approach uses the inverse of our verbalization lens to identify directions in activation space that the model decodes as a particular token. We can then subtract the latent vector that corresponds to "ant" from the image, and add in the latent vector corresponding to "iPod". See Section 4 in the full paper for details!

Related Work

Nostalgebraist. interpreting GPT: the logit lens. 2020.
This post first introduced the idea of logit lens, applying the LLM decoder head to intermediate hidden states. They found that it revealed a surprising amount of intermediate information about GPT's internal computations.

Nick Jiang, Anish Kachinthaya, Suzie Petryk, Yossi Gandelsman. Interpreting and Editing Vision-Language Representations to Mitigate Hallucinations. 2025.
Along with Neo et al. (2025), this paper first applied logit lens to image tokens in VLMs to understand their alignment with language. The authors showed that they could reduce hallucinations by subtracting directions corresponding to hallucinated objects from image tokens, similar to our editing approach. We use similar evaluations to theirs in our Section 3.

Clement Neo, Luke Ong, Philip Torr, Mor Geva, David Krueger, Fazl Barez. Towards Interpreting Visual Information Processing in Vision-Language Models. 2025.
Along with Jiang et al., this paper made the first foray into applying logit lens to image tokens. They show interesting qualitative examples of logit lens results for image tokens in late layers (whereas our method works across all layers).

Benno Krojer, Shravan Nayak, Oscar Mañas, Vaibhav Adlakha, Desmond Elliott, Siva Reddy, Marius Mosbach. LatentLens: Revealing Highly Interpretable Visual Tokens in LLMs. 2026.
This work provides a different way of visualizing image tokens by measuring cosine similarity with latent in-context representations of natural language (e.g., image captions). This approach results in much more interpretable image token labels, and we find is comparable to our method.

Sheridan Feucht, Eric Todd, Byron C. Wallace, David Bau. The Dual-Route Model of Induction. 2025.
We adapt the "Concept Lens" method from our own prior work. While they used this approach to understand concept induction heads in LLMs, here we apply it to image tokens in VLMs using a different set of attention heads.
